After Your GCSE & A-Level Results: Every Route Forward

6 min read · Published 2026-08-21 · Updated 2026-08-21

Results day rarely goes exactly to plan — and that is completely normal. Whether you smashed it, just missed out, or nothing went the way you hoped, there is a real route forward from every single outcome. This is your calm, no-panic map of what to do next after GCSE or A-Level results in England.

First: breathe, then act early

Two things are true at once: your results do not define you, and the days right after results day matter. Re-mark deadlines and university Clearing move fast, so it pays to know your options today. Free, confidential advice is a phone call away on the National Careers Service helpline: 0800 100 900.

If you didn't get the GCSE grades you needed

Missed a grade 4 in English or Maths?

If you are 16–18 you will keep studying English and Maths — it is a condition of post-16 funding. Most students resit at college (in November and again next summer) alongside their main course, or take Functional Skills Level 2, an accepted alternative for many jobs and apprenticeships. Enrol at a local college now; many take late applications in August and September. See GOV.UK: further education courses.

Think a grade is wrong?

If you were close to a boundary, your school can request a Review of Marking from the exam board — there is a fee, refunded if the grade changes, and priority reviews exist when a place depends on it. Deadlines are tight, so speak to your school's exams officer straight away. See GOV.UK: appeals and reviews.

Not sure what's next?

A college course at the right level (Level 1 or 2, BTEC/NVQ) builds real skills and progresses to Level 3, T-Levels or an apprenticeship. Or start an apprenticeship — you earn while you learn, with English and Maths built in (find an apprenticeship). Our Career Passport can help you weigh academic against vocational routes.

If you didn't get the A-Level grades you needed

Missed your university offer?

Call your firm or insurance university first — they may still accept you, or offer a foundation year or a related course. They often decide fast on results day. If you have no confirmed place, UCAS Clearing matches you to courses with spaces using the grades you actually got: log in to UCAS Hub, get your Clearing number, and phone universities directly. See how Clearing works (UCAS).

A place hinging on one or two marks?

Ask your school for a Priority Review of Results with the exam board — the university may hold your place while it is reviewed. Tell the university you have requested it.

Rethinking the whole plan?

There are strong routes that are not the traditional degree: a foundation year or an Access to HE Diploma (official site), a degree apprenticeship (a full degree paid for by an employer, no tuition debt), BTEC/HNC/HND qualifications that top up to a degree later, or a planned gap year to gain experience and reapply with confirmed grades. Resitting your A-Levels as a private candidate is also an option if grades are the only blocker.

Do I have to resit GCSE English and Maths if I failed?

If you are 16–18 and did not get a grade 4, you keep studying them as a condition of post-16 funding — usually by resitting at college or taking Functional Skills Level 2. It is not a punishment; a grade 4+ in both opens far more doors.

Can I still go to university if I missed my grades?

Often yes. Call your firm and insurance choices first — they may accept you anyway or offer a foundation year. If not, UCAS Clearing places thousands of students on good courses every year using their actual grades.

Is it too late to get my exam re-marked?

No, but act fast. Only your school can request a Review of Marking, deadlines fall within weeks of results, and priority reviews exist when a university place depends on the grade.

What are the alternatives to A-Levels and university?

Apprenticeships and degree apprenticeships, T-Levels and BTECs, HNC/HND qualifications, Access to HE and foundation years all lead to strong careers — sometimes faster and with no student debt.
