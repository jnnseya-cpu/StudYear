Spotting NEET risk from primary — without labelling a single child

5 min read · Published 2026-08-07 · Updated 2026-08-07

A government review into the NEET crisis — young people <strong>not in education, employment or training</strong> — is set to recommend that primary schools in England identify, early, the children most likely to leave education jobless at 16. It is the right instinct: by Year 11 the trajectory is often set. But it raises an obvious worry — how do you flag a seven-year-old's future without labelling them?

Early identification is only useful if it triggers support

Spotting risk is not the goal; acting on it is. The evidence from area-based approaches — the kind of cross-agency model the review is studying in Dublin — is that early, sustained mentoring and real encounters with the world of work change destinations. Identification without a response is just a label.

The signals are already in the building

Schools don't need a new assessment to see risk early. The most predictive signals are ones every primary already generates: attendance patterns, reading and number fluency, engagement and behaviour. The Education Endowment Foundation is clear that attendance and early literacy and numeracy are among the strongest levers for later outcomes.

Do it as strengths-and-needs, never a verdict

The safe way to run this matters as much as the maths:

  • Frame every child as on track, keep watch, or priority for support — never a prediction they will be unemployed, and never something the child sees.
  • Let context — Pupil Premium, SEND, EAL, attendance — unlock extra help, never lower a child's score. Otherwise identification just entrenches disadvantage.
  • Show the drivers, keep a teacher in the loop, monitor for any group being over-represented, and keep records permissioned and audit-logged.

FAQ

What does NEET mean?

NEET stands for "not in education, employment or training" — used for young people, typically 16–24, who are not in any of those. The review's aim is to reduce it by acting far earlier, in primary school.

Isn't it wrong to label a young child as a future NEET?

Yes — which is why it should never be done as a label. Done properly it is a strengths-and-needs view that widens support: the child is never told a prediction, and context only unlocks help rather than lowering their score.

How can primary schools identify risk without extra workload?

By computing it from data the school already generates — attendance, reading and number fluency, engagement and behaviour — and surfacing it inside tools staff already use, so spotting risk is a glance, not a new form.
